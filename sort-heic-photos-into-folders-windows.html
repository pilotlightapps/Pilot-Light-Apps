HEIC photo organization guide

Sort HEIC photos into folders on Windows without converting the originals first

iPhone libraries often mix HEIC photos, JPEG exports, videos, screenshots, and repeated backups. A useful organizer should show what it can read and account for everything it cannot.

Short answer: Keep the HEIC sources, use a local decoder for review, list unreadable variations instead of hiding them, and build new organized folders without changing the original library.

Why HEIC libraries can be awkward on Windows

HEIC and HEIF are efficient image formats commonly produced by Apple devices. Windows support can vary with the installed applications and codecs. A folder may therefore contain photos that preview normally beside files that show no thumbnail or refuse to open in a particular program.

Converting the entire library to JPEG may make viewing easier, but it also creates another set of files and can discard format-specific information or quality. Conversion should be a deliberate choice, not a hidden requirement for organization.

Account for the whole phone export

A phone backup is rarely photos only. It may include videos, sidecar files, edited copies, downloaded images, and damaged or unsupported variations. Before sorting, compare the scan report with the physical file count. Videos can remain outside a photo workflow while still being counted and listed by path.

Unreadable files should also remain visible. An organizer should report them and leave them untouched rather than silently excluding them from the final numbers.

Choose folders based on how you retrieve photos

Year and month folders are useful when capture dates are reliable. Event, person, place, project, and status categories are better when you search by meaning. Layered tags allow the same HEIC photo to appear in more than one organized output without forcing you to choose only one interpretation.

A backup-first HEIC workflow

  1. Preserve the phone export and make an independent backup.
  2. Begin with a copied or non-critical folder for the first test.
  3. Scan the complete folder tree and review photos, videos, exclusions, unreadable files, and exact duplicates.
  4. Sort in small, focused passes using categories you will actually search.
  5. Build new output folders and verify them before relying on the result.

How Picture Pilot handles HEIC and HEIF

Picture Pilot Sorter includes a local HEIC/HEIF decoder, so its photo review does not require Microsoft's optional photo codecs or an upload service. It also supports common photo formats, lists damaged or unsupported variations as unreadable, and counts videos even though the app remains photo-only.

The source photos are read but never moved, renamed, edited, overwritten, or deleted. Organized folders are built as new output. The current release is an unsigned Windows public test, so back up irreplaceable photos and independently verify the scan report and completed output.
